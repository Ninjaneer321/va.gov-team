## Research Goals

Primary goals:
- Identify top calculators clinicians are using and what is working well
- Understand barriers to use or friction points
- Identify new calculators that would support clinical workflows

Secondary goals:
- Identify more insights related to clinician workflows when using the tools

- What outcomes are we aiming to achieve for clinicians?  

We want to understand if clincians have the calculators they need and identify pain points in the workflow.

## Research Questions

**`1.`What are the top barriers or points of friction that impact the clinician's experience using CDS Medical Calculators?** 

**`2.`What improvements or new calculators would best support clinician's workflows?** 

**`3.`What are clinicians using today in CDS Medical Calculators and what is working well?**



## Methodology 
We conducted remote moderated research sessions (30-60-minutes) via MS Teams. Participants were asked questions about their role at the VA and their current or past use of the CDS Medical Calculator application. Then we asked the clinician questions in the conversation guide and had them share their screen if needed to walk through any issues or highlight features.
  

## Hypotheses and Conclusions

- Due to low usage of many calculators, we assume  clinicians do not have a large number of calculators that they regularly need or use. 
  
    **Somewhat true** For some clincians, Medical Calculators has the calculators they regularly use. For other clinicians, especially those focused on geriatrics, there are gaps in what calculators and risk assessments are available.
  
- There is low awareness of CDS tools and Medical Calculators.
  
    **True** Clinicians generally reported that they believe there is not a lot of awareness of CDS tools and Medical Calculators. Some were lucky to be introduced to it by a peer or mentor.
  
- Clinicians may need more decision support from our tool after calculating a score.
  
    **Somewhat false** While this has come up in other studies, this wasn't a main topic for the clinicians this time around since they were familiar with the calculators they used already.

- Clinicians would want more recommended calculators.  
  
    **Somewhat true** Clinicians did respond favorably to the idea of enhancing the ability to recommend the right calculator at the right time, however, most clinicians were unaware of the existing capability through the patient details. 


## Key Findings

1. Fragility affects everything, and it's the biggest gap in Medical Calculators.
2. Peer to peer sharing is more compelling for trying new tools.
3. Feature awareness is generally low for busy clinicians.
4. Autopopulation is not the only way calculators save time and add value.
5. Pain points varied from missing calculators to narrow autopopulation windows.



## Details of Findings 

### Fragility affects everything, and it's the biggest gap in Medical Calculators.
- General mortality and fragility assessments can provide clinicians with objective ways to discuss a patient's health and risks. For example, depending on a patient's mortality and life expectancy, a patient and provider may find that less aggressive cancer treatment better balances quality of life with the overall life expectancy. The conversation shifts from intensive and invasive cancer treatments or surgeries to a conversation around disease management that matches more how chronic conditions are managed and monitored to maintain quality of life and reduce pain. 
- Cognitive assessments at the point of care provide additional objective measures of the patient's health that could be repeated at future visits and provide a way of collecting longitudinal data to identify declines in cognitive functioning. 
- Surgery slips require a patient risk assessment (VASQIP) to be completed. This can take about 15 minutes since it doesn't autopopulate any data. Building this calculator could help reduce the time burden to complete. 

> Fragility affects everything. -Doctor of Nursing, Pulmonology

> There is increasing pressure to speed clinical decision making and issues such as life expectancy are hard to put a number on. Data backed tools that enable providers to assess with confidence and move forward would be of value. -Physician, Outpatient Oncology Clinic

> Even a standard Social Security Administration life expectancy chart, can help use with framing the conversation. -Physician, Urology & Surgery

### Peer to peer sharing is more compelling for trying new tools.
- While clinicians learned about CDS and Medical Calculators through a variety of channels, the peer to peer sharing was how most people learned about the tool. Peer to peer sharing took a few different forms:
-   Mentor - mentee relationships
-   Provider "tips & tricks" Teams channel.
-   Clinician created poster or powerpoint presentations.
- Peer to peer sharing can also diminish doubt that learning a new tool is cumbersome and won't really save time. They are learning from colleagues they trust. 

> My mentor when I started already was aware of the tools and showed me.  -Physician, Primary Care

> Look you'll save hours. I'll show him the patient details now with the recommended calcs. I'll show the pulmonologist. I'll show the cardiologist. -Physician, Primary Care

> There is little awareness of Medical Calculators. I did a poster presentation about it. Providers ask, "Is this really going to save me time." I tell them, "If I can do it, you can to. I'm like you and it's worth it. It's life changing. I don't leave 2 hours after my job is done, it's more like 30 minutes now." -Doctor of Nursing, Pulmonology

### Feature awareness is generally low for busy clinicians.
- Clinicians come into Medical Calculators to complete a very specific task and then move on to the next part of the workflow. This results in less exploration or noticing of features and functionality.
- Most clinicians were unaware of the "Patient Details" tab with recommended calculators, but all of them thought it was helpful and could see value in expanding the functionality.
- Some clinicians commented that the list of calculators is really long and it can take a while to scroll. After demoing Favorites functionality, clinicians thought that would really help.
- One clinician who is part of the Knowtex pilot felt she used Medical Calculators less because it was harder to navigate all of the different tools on the laptop. After demoing the side navigation of the console, and how multiple tools can be opened, she said "that's going to help a lot!"

> I only use CDS console for MedCalcs. I have MWW saved as a bookmark. For me if [opening the CDS Console] went straight to Patient Details with recommended calcs that wouldn't be a problem. -Physician, Primary Care

> I like that actually, that's really helpful. I'll probably use that tool. I didn't know that's what you could do. That's faster. That's probably what I'd look up anyway. I like that. (Patient Details with recommended calculators) -Physician

### Autopopulation is not the only way calculators save time and add value.
- While autopopulation of data is the most obvious way clinicians save time when using Medical Calculators, there are other ways calculators save clinician time and add value:
  - Standardized formulas and results help ensure clinicians are comparing apples to apples.
  - Having multiple formulas allow providers to more easily compare and contrast results.
  - The results reinforce the clinician's gestalt and make charting easier.
  - Clinicians don't need to search among many online calculators to find a vetted and evidenced based formula and guidance.

> I would want to know the calculators are data backed and would want more info for providers about what's available. Resources are going to be needed to help us be more efficient given the evolving productivity goals. Anything you can to to make resources, make us aware, and make sure the guidance is supported would be appreciated. -Physician Assistant

> I'm not sure if I just don't know what's out there. -Physician

### Pain points varied from missing calculators to narrow autopopulation windows.
- Some clinicians reported that the window for autopopulating labs was too narrow at 48 hours. While that timeframe works for the context of inpatient calculations, some of the calculators are still used in an outpatient context so 48-hour lookback periods were too restrictive.
- In addition to not having calculators related to geriatric assessments available, some clinicians reported missing PCCI after it was taken offline as well as PREVENT and other assessments.
- While some clinicians found copy to clipboard useful, others preferred different formats for documenting the result.
- When favoriting a lot of calculators, the favorites list can get too long to find what is needed most often.
- Similar to tracking results over time, sometimes a clinician wants to calculate what the result was 6 months or a year ago if the calculation wasn't done at that time.


> I want to know what the score would have been 6 months ago. sometimes the calc changes our true thought. -Physician, Primary Care

> Maybe we can make more note templates for standard language with how they write the note. -Doctor of Nursing

> I end up wanting to make it smaller so it won't take up too much space in my note.
-Physician, Primary Care

## Recommendations

1. Research and review geriatric related calculators to see what is possible to implement. 
2. Research and review different channels for engaging and advertising CDS tools to clinicians.
3. Provide easy to share resources so clinicians can forward videos or quick start guides to their colleagues.
4. Provide evidence or metrics where possible on how Medical Calculators saves time and helps with workflows.
5. Consider these listed channels of communications and see what might be broadly applicable across other sites:
    - VA leadership at site and VISN levels (or specialty and role based levels)
    - Site CHIO
    - MS Teams channel related to provider efficiency tips and tricks.
    - Employee onboarding processes to new tools and mentor-mentee programs
    - Communications between main campuses and satellite sites.
    - iEX conference
    - Announcements regarding new tools or guidelines (like the VHA/DoD Lipid guidelines now pointing to PREVENT)
    - Links in reminders can continue to be a new entry point (some found PCCI through the oncology pathway notes)
6. Create a short video tutorial and one-page resource highlighting the important features.
7. Evaluate tool onboarding and in-app feature notifications to see how effective it is with helping clinicians learn about application functionality.
8. Evaluate ways for clinicians to "opt-in" easily to quarterly emails about new functionality while in the application experience.
9. Research ways to identify "snapshot" data based on specialty. For example, what are the top ICD-10 billing codes used in urology or labs ordered? This could help with determining what data is most relevant according to a clinician's specialty. Consider how this data might differ if the setting changes (eg urology outpatient vs urology inpatient).
10. When evaluating new calculator requests, also consider if the calculator adds another layer of risk stratification before considering it a duplication and not added value to develop.
11. Consider calculators that don't autopopulate, but provide a standardized way for clinicians to track objective measures over time. For example, some point of care cognitive assessments might not autopopulate, but a clinician can complete the same one over time to compare apples to apples.
12. Consider ways clinicians can track and trend calculator results over time without having to search through notes for the last recorded result.
13. Research ways to adjust results copy to clipboard content to create more templated notes following the structure of how a clinician would write up the result.
13. Consider alternatives to creating a shorter copy to clipboard note for those who want a leaner version.
15. Provide custom sorting options for favorites - clinician's have their most commonly used favorites, and then other favorites that they want to easily access (over having to scroll or search), but they don't want those adding to the scroll to get to their commonly used ones. Explore ways to provide different sorting options of the favorites list, such as most frequently used or custom sorting.
16. Evaluate ways clinicians could pull in older data to calculate a historic result for comparison.
17. Increase the autopopulation window for calculators that have use beyond inpatient and leverage soft alerts instead of a hard restriction on data lookback.
